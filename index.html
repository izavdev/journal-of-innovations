Concept one-pager

Journal of Innovations

Every change is a hypothesis. Measure consistently, judge the evidence honestly, and preserve what you learn.

The idea in simple words

A shared log where every change to how we work (a new tool, step, rule or ritual) is recorded as a small experiment.

Before changing anything, we write down what we change, what we expect to happen and how we'll know. We measure before, make the change, and measure again in exactly the same way.

Why it matters

  • Changes are often introduced and assumed to help, but nobody checks.
  • Good ideas go unnoticed, and bad ones quietly stay in place.
  • Discussions rely on gut feeling instead of evidence.
  • The journal builds team memory of what works and what doesn't.
Hypothesis→Measure before→Implement→Measure after→Decide

How it works

  1. Describe the innovationWhat exactly are we changing?
  2. State the hypothesisWhat effect do we expect, and why?
  3. Pick the metricsWhich 1–3 numbers will show the effect?
  4. Fix the measurement methodData source, formula and time window
  5. Measure the "before"Record the baseline: where are we today?
  6. Implement, measure the "after"Compare, then keep, adjust or roll back

The golden rule

Use the same calculation method for "before" and "after": the same formula, data source, scope and length of time window. If the method changes, recompute both periods or mark the comparison as limited. Consistent measurement shows what changed; a stronger comparison is needed to isolate why.

Journal entry template

InnovationWhat is being introduced
Owner / startWho is responsible, and from when
Hypothesis"If we do X, metric Y will change by about Z, because…"
Metrics / whyOne primary outcome and 1–2 supporting or guardrail metrics; explain their relevance and limits
ExpectationPredicted magnitude and its basis, recorded before seeing results
MethodFormula, data source, time window (same before and after)
Success criteriaWhat counts as "it worked", set upfront
Before / AfterValues and the periods they cover
Comparison designBefore/after, comparison group or randomized trial; units, periods and assumptions
Alternative explanationsWhat else could explain the change, and how to check it
Evidence and limitsWhat was observed, what can be attributed, and what remains uncertain
DecisionKeep, adjust or roll back, plus learnings

Example: feeling versus evidence

InnovationA checklist for code reviews (synthetic example)
Expectation30% less waiting for a first review; basis: intuition
Metric / whyMean elapsed hours to first substantive review: measures author waiting, not mental effort
MethodSame team, two 28-day request cohorts, seven-day follow-up; cap each wait at 168 hours, including unreviewed requests
SuccessAt least 15% lower waiting; no increase in seven-day corrective-follow-up rate
Result20 hours before, 19 after: 1 hour / 5% lower. Target missed; guardrail unchanged in observed data
LimitsBefore/after only; request complexity unmeasured. Causal contribution and precision uncertain
DecisionKeep provisionally because upkeep is judged low; reassess under a new plan. Do not declare success

Good example / bad example

The same checklist, two ways to describe it. These are synthetic examples; a good entry can record a missed target.

Bad: a convincing story

“Reviews feel 30% faster. Forty reviews used the checklist, so it works. Waiting fell from 20 to 19 hours. Any improvement is a success—roll it out everywhere.”

Why it fails: feeling becomes a number, adoption becomes proof, and the original success threshold disappears. Other explanations go unchecked.

Good: a useful record

“We expected 30% less waiting and set a 15% minimum improvement upfront. With the same measurement method, waiting fell from 20 to 19 hours: 5%, so the target was missed. The observed quality guardrail was unchanged.”

“Before/after alone cannot isolate the checklist’s effect; request complexity was unmeasured. Keep provisionally because upkeep is judged low; the owner will reassess under a new plan.”

Why it works: expectation, measurement, uncertainty and decision remain distinct.

Keeping it honest

  • Write the hypothesis and success criteria first, before any results come in, so nobody can move the goalposts.
  • Reduce competing changes where possible, and record alternative explanations. One change at a time does not eliminate background trends.
  • Compare equal periods that are long enough to smooth out random ups and downs.
  • Keep perception separate from measurement. If you expected 30% but measured 5%, report 5%. A different benefit becomes a new hypothesis.
  • Log failures and inconclusive results too. A change that didn't work is still useful knowledge.
  • Watch for side effects. Improving one metric can make another one worse.

Match the claim to the evidence

Before / after: “The metric improved after the change.” Comparison group: “Our improvement exceeded the comparison group’s,” subject to comparable trends and conditions. Randomized trial: stronger causal evidence when assignment, collection and analysis are sound.

Choose a design proportionate to the decision. Separate the practical choice to keep something from confidence that it caused an improvement. A small observed difference alone establishes neither a reliable benefit nor no effect.

Use the approach, in your own tools

This is a concept and toolkit, not an app. Keep your journal wherever your team works.
